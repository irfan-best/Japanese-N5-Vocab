nyuuyooku wa nanji desu ka
vs
nyuuyooku de nanji desu ka
    The correct choice is nyuuyooku wa nanji desu ka (にゅーよーくは なんじですか).

    Here is why は (wa) works and で (de) does not in this specific sentence structure:

    1. にゅーよーくは なんじですか (Correct)
    Topic Marker (は): Sets "New York" as the main topic you are asking about.

    Literal meaning: "As for New York, what time is it?"

    When to use: Whenever you want to ask the current time in a specific city or region.

    2. にゅーよーくで なんじですか (Incorrect)
    Action Location Marker (で): Marks the place where an action takes place (e.g., "I ate lunch at the restaurant").

    Why it doesn't work: "Is" / "What time is it?" is a state of being, not an active event or action, so で cannot attach directly to なんじですか.

Words like kesa(this morning), kinou no yoru(yesterday night), 毎日 (mainichi), 明日 (ashita), 今日 (kyou), 昨日 (kinou), 毎朝 (maiasa), etc. are time expressions. In Japanese, time expressions do not need the particle に (for these common words), and they are usually placed near the beginning of the sentence, before the topic.
    The most common sentence order is:
    Time + Topic (は) + Place (で/へ/に) + Object (を) + Verb

    event Topic (wa) + Time + ... -> is also correct.

Sakki vs sakini:
    While both Sakini (先に) and Sakki (さっき) derive from the root word saki (先 - meaning "ahead" or "front"), they refer to completely different directions of time and usage.

    1. Sakini (先に) — Ahead, First, Beforehand
        Used to describe doing something first in sequence, before someone else or before another action. It points forward in priority or time.

        O-saki ni douzo
        Go ahead of me. / After you.

    2. Sakki (さっき) — Just now, A moment ago
        Used as a time adverb pointing to the very recent past (usually a few minutes to an hour ago).

        Sakki dareka kita?
        Did someone come just a moment ago?

Tonari vs Yoko:
    1. 隣 (Tonari) — Next to (Same Category / Immediate Neighbor)
        Use 隣 (tonari) when two items are side-by-side and belong to the same category or share a similar nature (e.g., person to person, house to house, chair to chair).

        Strict Requirement: They must be immediately adjacent with nothing similar in between.

        Key Concept: Same type of thing.

        Examples:

        Tanaka-san no tonari (田中さんの隣) = Next to Mr. Tanaka (person next to a person).

        Gakkou no tonari (学校の隣) = Next to the school (a building/store right next to a school).

        Tonari no seki (隣の席) = The seat next to mine.

        2. 横 (Yoko) — Beside / Side (Position & Alignment)
        Use 横 (yoko) to describe physical position along a horizontal axis (left or right). The items do not need to belong to the same category.

        Flexibility: Works for completely different types of objects (e.g., a bag next to a chair, a trash can next to a vending machine).

        Key Concept: Horizontal positioning (side / lateral area).

        Examples:

        Tsukue no yoko (机の横) = Beside the desk / at the side of the desk.

        Teke-bu no yoko ni gomi-bako ga arimasu (テレビの横にごみ箱があります。) = There is a trash can beside the TV. (TV and trash can are different categories, so yoko is used).

    Totemo vs Zuibun:
        1. とても (Totemo) — Simple / Objective "Very"
    とても (totemo) is a neutral, straightforward way to intensify an adjective or verb. It simply means "very," "really," or "extremely."

    Key Nuance: Neutral, factual, and objective.

    When to use: Anytime you want to say something is "very [adjective]" without adding emotional surprise.

    Examples:

    Kyou wa totemo atsukui desu. (今日はとても熱いです。)

    Today is very hot. (Stating a plain fact)

    Kono ramen wa totemo oishii desu. (このラーメンはとても美味しいです。)

    This ramen is very delicious.

    2. ずいぶん (Zuibun) — "Surprisingly / Remarkably / More Than Expected"
    ずいぶん (zuibun) carries an element of surprise, unexpectedness, or personal impression. It implies that the degree of something exceeded your expectations or represents a noticeable change over time.

    Key Nuance: "Surprisingly," "considerably," "quite a lot (more than I thought)."

    When to use: When you are impressed, surprised, or pointing out a contrast/change.

    Examples:

    Kyou wa zuibun atsukui desu ne. (今日はずいぶん熱いですね。)

    It's remarkably hot today, isn't it? (Implies it's much hotter than usual or hotter than expected)

    Nihongo ga zuibun jouzu ni narimashita ne. (日本語がずいぶん上手になりましたね。)

    Your Japanese has become surprisingly good! (Highlights noticeable progress over time)

Getsu・Sui・Kin         vs      Getsuyoubi to Suiyoubi to Kinyoubi
    1. Getsu・Sui・Kin (月・水・金)
    Usage Context: Schedules, timetables, and casual or concise speech.

    Why it's used: It is a shorthand abbreviation. In Japanese, when listing multiple days of the week, speakers drop the repeating ~youbi (曜日 - day of the week) and the particle to (と - and).

    Tone: Efficient, concise, and natural when discussing recurring routines, schedules, or calendars.

    2. Getsuyoubi to Suiyoubi to Kinyoubi (月曜日と水曜日と金曜日)
    Usage Context: Formal speech, precise announcements, or full structural sentences.

    Why it's used: It uses the full name of each day (Getsuyoubi, Suiyoubi, Kinyoubi) connected explicitly by the list-connecting particle to ("and").

    Tone: Clear and complete, but in fast everyday conversation, repeating ~youbi to three times can feel wordy or overly formal.

choushi ga warui        vs      kibun ga warui:
    Core Difference
    調子（ちょうし） (Choushi) refers to condition, state, tone, or operation. It can refer to physical health, mental state, or even machines and overall performance.

    気分（きぶん） (Kibun) refers specifically to feelings, emotion, or temporary bodily sensations (like nausea or feeling sick to your stomach).

    1. 調子が悪い (Choushi ga warui)
    Primary Meaning: "To be out of shape," "to feel under the weather," "to not function well."

    When to use it:

    General Physical Health: You have a mild cold, fatigue, or feel generally off/under the weather.

    Performance / Condition: You aren't playing sports well, or a machine/computer is glitching.

    Longer-term or broad state: Overall health or routine isn't going smoothly.

    Examples:

    体調が悪い。 (Taichou ga warui.) — "My physical condition is bad." (Slightly more formal/specific to body).

    パソコンの調子が悪い。 (Pasokon no choushi ga warui.) — "My computer isn't working right."

    今日は調子が悪い。 (Kyou wa choushi ga warui.) — "I'm not feeling 100% today / I'm off my game today."

    2. 気分が悪い (Kibun ga warui)
    Primary Meaning: "To feel sick/nauseous" OR "to feel offended/upset."

    When to use it:

    Physical Sensation: You feel dizzy, nauseous, motion sick, or like you might throw up.

    Emotional Reaction: Someone said something rude and it ruined your mood or made you angry/offended.

    Examples:

    吐き気がして、気分が悪い。 (Hakike ga shite, kibun ga warui.) — "I feel nauseous and sick."

    車酔いで気分が悪い。 (Kuruma-yoi de kibun ga warui.) — "I feel sick from motion sickness."

    彼の言葉を聞いて気分が悪くなった。 (Kare no kotoba o kiite kibun ga waruku natta.) — "Hearing his words made me feel upset / offended."

hokano vs betsu no
    Hokano (他の): Noun + Particle no (の). Hoka (他 / 外) is a noun meaning "other," "besides," or "the rest."

    Betsu no (別の): Noun + Particle no (の). Betsu (別) is a noun meaning "different," "separate," or "another."

    Hokano (他の): Means "other" in the sense of "in addition to" or "besides this one." It implies choosing another option from a set of remaining choices, or looking at "the rest."

    Betsu no (別の): Means "another" in the sense of "different", "distinct", or "separate." It emphasizes a complete change in quality, type, category, or location.

Shuuri vs Naoshi:
    Shūri (修理): Formal, technical, and mechanical. Used for fixing broken machinery, electronics, vehicles, structures, or complex systems to restore them to working order.

    Naoshi (直し): Casual, general, and broad. Used for altering, adjusting, touching up, or correcting clothes, mistakes, habits, or minor physical defects.
